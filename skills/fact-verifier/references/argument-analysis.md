# Argument Analysis and Potential Fallacies

Analyze only arguments actually made by the material. Do not attribute a search result or commenter's view to the article under examination. For each argument, record a locatable quotation or passage, `facts` (factual assertions made by the source, with separate independent-verification status), `assumptions` (implicit premises and their evidential support), `inference` (how the premises are supposed to lead to the conclusion), `conclusion` (preserving the source's degree of certainty), and `type`. When reviewing an article, distinguish its author's central argument from arguments made by people it quotes.

| Type | Key question |
| --- | --- |
| Deductive | Would the conclusion necessarily follow if the premises were true? Are the factual premises actually sound? Validity does not establish premise truth. |
| Inductive | How was the sample obtained, does it represent the population, and does the wording exceed what the sample supports? |
| Abductive | Were the main alternative explanations considered? Which account best explains or predicts the observed facts? |
| Statistical | Are denominators, definitions, baselines, intervals, selection bias, and absolute versus relative risk clear? |
| Causal | Are temporal order, comparison groups, confounders, reverse causality, mechanism, and counterfactuals addressed? |
| Analogical | Are the similarities relevant to the conclusion, and have important differences been omitted? |

A valid deduction with true premises yields a true conclusion. Reasonable induction and abduction generally strengthen a conclusion without making it logically certain. Explain factual reliability and inferential strength separately. Mark a first-hand record that makes no explicit argument as `argumentQuality: not_applicable`.

For a potential fallacy, describe the specific inferential leap, quote a relevant short passage, explain its effect on the particular claim, and label it `potential` rather than declaring it conclusive without context. Check especially:

| Candidate issue | Missing condition to examine |
| --- | --- |
| Circular reasoning | Does the premise merely restate the conclusion, or is there independent support? |
| Straw man | Is the attacked version materially different from the other person's actual statement? |
| Hasty generalization | Do the sample size and sampling method justify generalization? |
| Slippery slope | Is there evidence or a mechanism for each relevant causal link? |
| False dichotomy | Have other plausible options really been ruled out? |
| Appeal to authority | Is the authority relevant, with checkable grounds and disclosed interests? Consulting an expert is not inherently fallacious. |
| Ad hominem | Are personal traits used instead of assessing the specific evidence? A relevant conflict of interest is not automatically irrelevant. |
| Post hoc ergo propter hoc | Is temporal succession treated as sufficient proof of causation? |
| Quote mining | Would omitted wording change the conditions, tone, or referent? |

If original context is missing, say “insufficient context to decide.” A conclusion can still be true when one offered argument is weak; a well-formed argument can still depend on a false premise. Explain what additional information would resolve the issue instead of merely listing fallacy names.
